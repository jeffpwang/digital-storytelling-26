SNAIL DISPLACEMENT — BUILD & PLANNING DOC

A hand-tracked interactive short. Starts as a data-viz science piece about snail dispersal, breaks form to reveal a single displaced snail, then breaks form again to reveal the real protagonist: a boy in a library who's been daydreaming the whole thing. The interaction vocabulary itself evolves with the story — familiar gestures become unpredictable ones, then game controls, then world-navigation.

Standalone experiment. Not bound to the memoir's specific visual language, but thematically a sibling to it. Doubles as a sneak-peek of what my larger interactive project will feel like.

---

THE SPINE (why this works)

Two nested reveals that rhyme:
- Snail data → one snail (the clinical narrator betrays itself, a story was hiding in the numbers)
- One snail → one boy (the animal was a kid's daydream all along)

Both reveals are the same move: the FORM breaks to show a person was inside it. That doubling is the whole engine. Every design decision should protect those two ruptures.

Core principle carried from my other work: agency granted, then stripped. The user is given control that feels innocent and productive, and the piece turns that control into the cause of harm and then takes it away. Failure should be something the user commits, not something they watch happen.

---

THE INTERACTION ARC (mechanic-as-meaning across the whole piece)

The control-feel changes at every beat, and each shift IS a story beat:
1. Familiar / simple — drag to scroll, pinch to zoom. Comfortable, almost mind-numbingly so. The user might even wonder why hands are needed for a dry science piece.
2. Expressive / tuning — two-hand spiral generator. The user feels like a DJ/mixer, real authorship.
3. Unpredictable / complicit — the familiar gesture turns out to be causing damage. Control becomes danger.
4. Game / failure — trying to join a flow the controls won't let you join.
5. World-navigation — first-person, choosing a direction but not the outcome.

Familiar → expressive → complicit → failing → crossing. That progression is the story told purely through how the hands feel.

FULL VISION — BEAT BY BEAT

BEAT 1 — THE WIDE VIEW (familiar control)
Top-down view of a large landscape. Clinical fact-card register. The user pinches outward to zoom; dragging pans. Fact introduced: rate of dispersal, shown across a wide area. The view rewards zooming — each zoom pulls toward a wooded patch, then a rock, the typical place snails are found.

Control feel: familiar, safe. This is the "why are we even using our hands for a data-viz piece?" beat. That mild confusion is intentional setup.

BEAT 2 — THE DISPERSAL FACT (familiar control continues)
As the user keeps zooming, they arrive at the snail scale. Fact: snails have a dispersal range of roughly 1–5 meters. (Verify exact figure/wording myself before locking.) Now we're in the zoomed-in snail world.

BEAT 3 — EVOLUTION ON A HUMAN SCALE (transition mechanic)
Fact: because the radius is so small, you can see evolutionary divergence on a human scale — patterns that normally need continents. Visual: a slow horizontal traverse where a microscope-circle stays centered and the world slides underneath it, shell patterns recombining as you move. Bounded, continuous, no cuts — the "small radius" idea expressed as camera grammar.

The variation reads as DIALECT, not decoration. Small grammar changes in a shared language. Built procedurally from shell parameters (whorl count, expansion rate, band spacing, pigment hue) — drift one or two parameters at a time across terrain. Each rock is a stable local cluster. Optionally, band color faintly echoes the local environment (mossy rock → green bands, clay → ochre) so "where you're from" is literally written on the shell.

The traverse dwells on one cluster: the protagonist's rock / tribe.

BEAT 4 — THE SPIRAL GENERATOR (expressive control)
The hand becomes a shell generator. Move it to control parameters — like a slider you can push back and forth, not a random roll, so the user feels authorship. Add a second hand and more properties unlock; a DJ/mixer feeling.

NARRATIVE JOB (important — not just a toy): the user is tuning the protagonist's shell to MATCH its home rock. The rock has a native pattern; the user adjusts until the snail belongs. The reward is achieving fit. This matters later: when displaced, the lovingly-tuned shell becomes the wrong one, and the generator gesture no longer responds. The thing made to create belonging becomes the mark of not-belonging, and control over it is gone. (Vestigial shell + agency-stripping in one mechanic.)

BEAT 5 — THE ACCIDENTAL RUPTURE (control becomes complicity) — THE FIRST "WOW"
The single best idea in the piece. Protect it.

The same gesture that felt productive becomes destructive. The pinch-zoom that's been innocently exploring is actually applying force — each zoom presses. Around the third or fourth zoom, a hairline crack appears at the edge of frame. The user, focused on the snail, probably doesn't notice. One more zoom — the crack gives. Water/earth floods in. The snail is swept away.

The horror is retroactive: "I was doing this the whole time." Accidental complicity, not a deliberate "break the dam" button — deliberate breaking lets the user off the hook; accidental complicity is the gut-punch. It also quietly makes the user the human force that displaces the snail.

FORM-BREAK: the microscope-circle that has framed every beat shatters / whips off-center. The controlled top-down view tumbles into chaotic moving POV. The clinical narrator is knocked out of the frame — no fact-card here, maybe silence or just rumble/wind. The omniscient voice can't follow the snail into this.

BEAT 6 — TRYING TO JOIN THE FLOW (game control / committed failure)
New world, new rock, native snails with a different native pattern. The displaced snail's tuned shell now mismatches. The generator gesture no longer responds.

Mini-game framing: NOT "avoid bumping." The goal the user is given is to JOIN the flow — match the pace and path of the line of native snails. The controls make it nearly impossible: the displaced snail moves on a foreign rhythm (different acceleration curve / input feel — foreign-tuned, NOT disabled). The user tries to belong and the mechanics won't allow it. Structural failure, not stated sadness.

Timelapse runs behind it (day/night lighting cycling) to show this happening across days. After enough failed attempts to join, the flow continues without the snail; camera pulls to leave it alone in frame. Linger here.

BEAT 7 — THE BOY REVEAL (world-navigation control) — THE SECOND "WOW"
BOOM — format shift. Frame may start small/portrait and expand to full screen. We meet the real protagonist: a boy, asian, awkward, who lives in books and his imagination.

The reveal mechanic: it starts inside the same small bounded frame the snail lived in, so we don't realize we've left the snail yet — then the frame expands to fill the screen as he snaps out of the daydream. "Small bounded world → suddenly the larger, harder real world" is one continuous gesture across both characters.

He's at a library. A PA announcement says the library closes in 10 minutes — this is the boy's catalyst, the human-scale version of the snail being lifted off its rock. The library is his safe rock; he's about to be removed from it.

First-person: a library book about snails is open in front of him. Use hands to close the book. Underneath is a homework worksheet with "Jeffrey Wang," the class, and the date — the quiet reveal that the whole nature documentary was a kid's homework-daydream. Let the name do the work; don't over-egg it.

It becomes a 3D world: control where the camera looks with the hand. Two clear options: back to the shelves, or out the door to the real world. The user picks; the camera moves forward in that direction.

BEAT 8 — THE CROSSING (the bookend)
Perspective changes once more — back to something we already know: the third-person-behind view we last had on the snail. But now it's on the boy. He faces a threshold (the door / the world), about to cross into somewhere unfamiliar, head-on.

THE THREAD: both stories end on the same camera grammar — third-person-behind, facing a threshold, the user given a look-direction but the crossing inevitable. The rhyme lands without a word. Snail-facing-new-world = boy-facing-new-world. End on the edge of the crossing. That edge is the sneak-peek: the real project is what happens after he's pushed out.

=========================================================
THE 30-SECOND CUT (this week's deliverable)
=========================================================

Segment: Zoom → fact → spiral-tune → accidental rupture → sweep. End on chaos.

This is a complete Snyder bookend on its own: calm controlled world → user unknowingly causes the break → displacement. It works as a teaser that makes people want the rest. The boy reveal stays as the unspoken promise of what's coming.

Rough timing (target ~30s, flexible):
- 0–6s — Wide top-down view. Pinch out to zoom. Fact: dispersal across a wide area. Zoom pulls toward a wooded patch → a rock.
- 6–12s — Arrive at snail scale. Fact: 1–5 meter dispersal range. (Verify figure.)
- 12–18s — Slow traverse, microscope-circle centered, world sliding under it, shell patterns drifting (dialect not decoration). Dwell on the protagonist's rock.
- 18–24s — Spiral generator: tune the shell to match the home rock. Achieving fit = the reward. Keep it short here; this is a taste of the mechanic, not the full DJ version.
- 24–30s — Keep zooming. Hairline crack at frame edge (most users won't notice). One zoom too many → crack gives → flood → snail swept. Microscope-frame shatters, view tumbles to chaotic POV. Cut to black on the chaos.

What to cut for the short version:
- No join-the-flow mini-game.
- No boy reveal, no library, no 3D navigation.
- Spiral generator is a brief taste, not the full two-hand mixer.

What MUST survive in the cut (or it doesn't land):
- The familiar-control setup (so the rupture means something).
- The accidental nature of the rupture (the user causes it without realizing).
- The form-break at the end (microscope-frame shatters / view tumbles).

Deliverable format: build as a self-contained HTML/CSS/JS piece with MediaPipe hands. Screen-record a clean playthrough as the 30-second video to hand in. The recording is the assignment; the interactive is the bonus / sneak-peek.

TECH NOTES
- MediaPipe Hands for gesture input: pinch (zoom), drag (pan), spiral motion (generate), two-hand (extra parameters).
- Procedural shells: logarithmic spiral from parameters (whorl count, expansion rate, band spacing, hue). Canvas or SVG. Genuinely generative, not faked.
- Microscope-circle frame is a persistent visual element from beat 1 — its shattering in beat 5 is the payoff, so establish it early and keep it consistent.
- Build in stages: get the zoom + fact-card flow working first, then the shell generator, then the rupture. The rupture depends on the zoom mechanic already existing, so it comes last.
- Style for the boy (later stage): flat-but-warmer illustration or a single AI-generated library still with a subtle camera push will sell the format-shift without a 3D/photoreal production black hole. The SHIFT sells the reveal, not the rendering tech.
